The ground game

Getting FAR in front of blokes in real life, without spending money we do not have and without anyone copping a fine. Written 27 Aug. A second, deeper research round is still running and this page gets updated when it lands.

Start here, because it changes the order of everything

Last month, 2,463 people looked at the FAR Instagram profile. 136 of them tapped the link. So 2,327 blokes went to the trouble of opening our profile and then left.

That is not a "nobody has heard of us" problem. That is a "they heard of us, had a look, and nothing made them act" problem. Which means the first job is fixing the page and the profile, not standing at a gate handing out stickers.

So the order is
1. Fix the product page and the bio. 2. Put the boots on Grant's and Lionel's feet on real sites. 3. THEN spend a few hundred dollars on printed stuff, with a number written down beforehand that tells us whether it worked.

Grant and Lionel: the bit no competitor can buy

Wear them. Film the reactions.

GrantLionelYou two stand on real sites and real depots every day. No brand with a marketing budget can buy that. Nobody can send a rep where you already are.

Two minutes a day. Fifteen seconds to camera at the ute before shift with the day count. Twenty seconds every time a bloke asks what they are.

Hold off until one thing lands. Fil needs written confirmation from the factory that the certificate covers our exact boot. Wearing an unconfirmed sample as safety gear on a live deck is our problem, not yours, and there is no insurance behind it yet. It is one email.

The toolbox talk

GrantEvery commercial site in the country runs a five to ten minute talk before shift, and footwear is a standard topic. You stand in one every single morning. That is the highest concentration of our exact customer that exists anywhere in Australia, it is free, and no outside brand gets in the room.

The play is not a pitch. It is writing something genuinely useful, one page on how to read a boot spec so a young bloke does not get done on his first pair, and it getting handed around by supervisors.

Your own smoko rooms

GrantLionelPosters go up on sites you are already inducted on, with the foreman's okay. Then you each hand a few to blokes you know on other sites.

Hard rule: nobody walks onto a site they are not inducted on. The old version of this plan had Fil doing 6:30am gate drops. He has no site access. That was wrong and it is fixed.

Lionel and the council thing

LionelBefore you go anywhere near a council contact, put your part-ownership of FAR in writing to your GM. Council rules make that a standing declaration whether or not you ever pitch to them. It protects you, and it protects the brand when someone brings it up later.

Then the useful job: pull the boot policy for six Sydney councils. They are public documents. We think there is a clause that lets an outdoor worker buy his own boots and claim the money back. If it exists in Sydney, that is a story worth telling: "your council will pay for your boots and you did not know."

The Bunnings box idea

Fil's version: our own cut-open branded boxes with a QR code, dropped into as many Bunnings box bays as we can get to, with a prize in one box out of every few hundred. Free boots, or the whole crew pack.

That is a different idea to the one that got knocked back, which was about riding on Bunnings' name. This is a treasure hunt using our own boxes.

It is being properly researched right now: the prize-draw permit rules are different in every state, and there is a question about whether a FAR box sitting in their store reads like we are partnered with them. Bunnings has owned Blackwoods, Hard Yakka and KingGee since July, so that matters more than it used to. Answer coming, with the conditions that would make it clean.

What we are spending, and the number that kills it

A few hundred dollars, not the thousand-plus the old plan had. Decals for the three utes, a couple of hundred stickers instead of five thousand, twenty posters instead of a hundred, and some chalk.

Small on purpose. Every printed thing carries its own web address so we can see exactly how many people came from it. Then we know, instead of guessing.

Written down before we spend a cent, signed by all three
Twenty posters, thirty days. If fewer than ten people scan them, the other eighty do not get printed and the sticker order does not go in.

The reason all three of us sign it: the bloke who spent his Saturday putting posters up is the last person on earth able to call it dead.

And we film the measuring, not the stunt. Grant pinning a poster, eight seconds. Thirty days later, Fil reading the real number out loud, whatever it is. If it is four scans, four scans is the video. A brand that publishes its own failed experiment earns something a brand that only publishes wins never can.

Do not do these. Two of them are actually illegal.

  • Nothing under a windscreen wiper. Ever. It is an offence in NSW, and whoever ASKS someone else to do it cops it worse than the person doing it.
  • No stickers on pub walls, poles, hoardings or shop windows. Also an offence, same deal about whoever authorised it. The old plan had 200 stickers going in pub bathrooms. That line is dead.
  • Chalk on a footpath is fine. Chalk-effect spray paint is not. Real chalk on a public footpath is specifically carved out of the law. A spray can turns it into a much bigger charge.
  • Never stick a decal on someone else's ute yourself, and never pay anyone to run one. Hand it over and let them do it. Paying turns a mate into a paid advertiser he has not declared, and that is the ACCC's business.
  • No fake reviews. None. Not from us, not from family, not from a mate we gave free boots to. An undisclosed freebie behind a review is illegal, and the ACCC has already fined a small business for exactly that this year.

Three things in the old plan that were just wrong

  • Blackwoods is off the list. Bunnings bought them in July, along with Hard Yakka and KingGee. We would be walking our stuff into a competitor's shop.
  • The FXD TAFE story we were copying is not what happened. They gave out shorts and socks, not boots, and they got in the door through a formal mentoring program, not by turning up.
  • Five thousand stickers was the wrong order. Wrong glue for hard hats, and a sticker is a badge for someone who already owns the thing. There is nothing to own yet.
