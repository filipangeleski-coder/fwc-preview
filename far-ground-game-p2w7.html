The uncomfortable bit first

The first version of this page said the problem was people opening our profile and not tapping the link. That was wrong, and it is worth showing why, because the real answer is more useful.

Last month: 285,702 people saw FAR content. 2,463 of them opened the profile. 136 tapped the link.

The maths that settles it: getting one extra person in a hundred to open the profile is worth about 158 extra clicks. Squeezing the link tap from 5.5% up to 8% is worth about 61. The upstream problem is worth roughly two and a half times the downstream one.

What that actually means
Loads of people see FAR and scroll past without ever tapping the name. That is a content problem, not a poster problem, and no amount of stickers touches it.

The ground game is still job three. But the reason is that the money and the hours are better spent upstream, not that the link is broken. And the Instagram bio still gets one edit this week: "Australia's comfiest work boot" comes out, because we cannot prove it on a boot nobody has worn yet and that is exactly the kind of claim we take other brands apart for. The "4 Mates" line stays.

And a number nobody wants to hear

Our biggest post ever was the Old Fella at about 4.8 million views, and Instagram removed it. The biggest one still standing is the Micromanager at 2.89 million.

The Micromanager got us 239 followers off those 2.89 million views. That is roughly ten times worse than an average FAR post at turning views into follows. Huge reach, almost nobody stayed.

And nobody has ever checked whether the people watching our comedy are blokes who buy work boots. That is a free test, about an hour: a poll on the story asking what people do for work, twenty DMs to recent commenters, and a look at the audience breakdown Instagram already gives us. Stickers $60-90Fil + Grant

200 to 300, not 5,000. The old plan said five thousand. That was wrong twice over.

  • Wrong glue. Normal sticker adhesive is solvent-based and solvent eats the plastic a hard hat is made of. The spec has to say pressure-sensitive vinyl with a NON-solvent adhesive, or every sticker either falls off or damages the hat.
  • Wrong placement. Outer shell only. Never over the brim edge, never over the certification stamp. A sticker covering the safety marking on a hard hat gets a bloke pulled up at induction.
  • Wrong timing. A sticker is a badge for someone who already owns the thing. There is nothing to own until December. The real sticker order goes in the boot box.
  • Missing design. There are eight concepts and not one of them says Formworker. That is Grant's own trade and the one we have direct access to.

The Reddit story everyone quotes, $500 of stickers built a billion-visit website, does not hold up as a reason to print five thousand. They had a product people already used.

Posters $15-25Grant3-4 hrs

Twenty A3s, not a hundred. One design, one web address (/site), QR code at least 4cm square with the address typed underneath as well, because half the blokes on site have a cracked screen or a locked-down work phone.

Grant pins them in smoko rooms on sites he is already inducted on, with the foreman's okay. Thirty days, then we read the number.

Ute decals $250-400All three6-9 hrs

Fifty large die-cut decals. We stick them on our own utes in a driveway on a Saturday, filmed start to finish, stuff-ups included. That is the video.

Decals, not a wrap. A full wrap counts as changing the vehicle's colour, which means telling Transport for NSW and telling your insurer. A decal does not. If Grant's ute belongs to his employer, magnets instead.

Then the other forty-odd go out one at a time to blokes we actually know. No ask, no code, no pitch. Eight weeks later we walk the same carparks and count how many are still on.

Two hard rules
Never put a decal on someone else's vehicle yourself. And never pay anyone to run one. Paying turns a mate into an advertiser who has not declared it, and that is illegal, not just dodgy.

Chalk stencils at site gates $80-220Grant10-12 hrs

Two cut stencils, real chalk, on the public footpath outside site gates between 5 and 6:30am. Short line plus "search FAR Workwear".

This is the lowest-risk thing on the whole page. The NSW graffiti law specifically carves out marking a public footpath with chalk. It is legal, written into the Act.

Two conditions and they both matter. Actual chalk, not chalk-effect spray, because a spray can turns a $440 maximum into $2,200 and up to twelve months. And public footpath only, never a private forecourt or a hoarding.

Reddit, and the clock starts now freeGrant + Lionel1 hr/wk x 4

Both of you make accounts and spend four weeks answering formwork, concrete, waste and council questions in the Australian renovation and Sydney subreddits. Four weeks, ten to twenty real comments each, zero mention of FAR.

Not politeness, the law
Posting about your own brand without saying it is yours is illegal under Australian consumer law. The ACCC fined a small business $39,600 in March this year for exactly that class of thing, and the maximum penalties went up sharply on 28 March. Disclose, every time.

Why start now: Reddit is the single most-quoted source when someone asks ChatGPT or Google a question. The four-week clock has to start immediately for week five to land while the pre-sale is open.

Cutting open dead boots on camera $0-250Grant fronting, Fil producing

The Bunnings box idea: the verdict

Fil's version: our own branded boxes with a QR code, dropped into Bunnings box bays, prize in one box out of a few hundred.

Verdict, revised 27 Aug: GO, with conditions. Ask the store first, never name them in a caption, and the prize cannot be boots.

REVISED 27 Aug. Fil pulled this up and he was right on the central point.

The first version of this said no, and leaned on "an unauthorised promotion inside a competitor's store". That framing was too strong, and the reason is simple once you look at what the box bay actually is.

The free boxes at Bunnings are supplier cartons. They are the boxes their stock arrived in, put out for customers instead of bags. Every single box in that bin already carries somebody's branding. That is the normal, expected state of that bin.

So a FAR-branded carton sitting in it is not an anomaly. It is indistinguishable from what belongs there. That is not a fake shelf ticket in an aisle, which is what the original comparison implied, and the comparison was wrong.

Fil's design makes it weaker still, in a good way: subtle black box, QR, FAR on the inside. You would not know until you picked it up or got it home. That is not a shopfloor advertisement at all.

What actually survives, and it is only two things

  • One thing genuinely unverified: whether Bunnings accepts boxes dropped OFF. Their own material covers giving boxes away, not taking them in, and no official donation program turned up. This is a two-minute question at the service desk and it converts the whole thing from a grey area into a permissioned tactic. Ask before you drop. Photograph the yes.
  • The risk is not the box. It is the caption. Naming the retailer is where the legal exposure lives, and it is real: a company copped a $1M penalty over ads that implied an association purely from context, without ever claiming one. A quiet branded box in a bin of branded boxes implies nothing. Us posting "go find them at Bunnings" is the thing that creates the association, and our own captions become the evidence. Never name the retailer, in anything.

What I got wrong, said plainly

The competitor-ownership point is still true — Bunnings has owned Blackwoods, Hard Yakka and KingGee since July — but I used it to argue the box would read as an unauthorised competitor promotion, and a branded carton in a bin of branded cartons does not read as anything. The ownership matters for whether we ever pitch them as a stockist. It does not make a cardboard box a provocation.

The graveyard-digging and heritage-site examples were also the wrong comparison class. Those failed because they sent people to dig in cemeteries. Nobody is digging anything.

The scale, so nobody oversells it

H&M Poland ran a well-executed hunt across their own store network. 200 people actually played. 943 new followers.

And the honest scan maths: the median on-pack QR code gets 21 scans in its entire life, across 46.9 million scans and 28 brands. 100 boxes out, maybe half get seen, 10 to 15% scan: about 5 to 8 scans. One FAR comedy reel does 20,000 views.

The box is a content piece and physical proof we exist. It is not a reach play and it must never be sold to us as one.

The mechanic has to change, and this is the good part

Do not put the winning token in a box. Every box identical, same QR, a scan is an entry, winner drawn on a published date.

  • Kills any argument about instant-prize rules.
  • Every state requires all prizes actually get distributed. If the winning box gets binned at 7am we are in breach and we cannot even tell.
  • It captures emails, which is our actual bottleneck. A scan-to-enter draw harvests name, email, trade, boot size, current brand and what killed it. A token in a box gets us one winner and zero information.

The condition that decides the timing

We cannot advertise a boot as a prize on a published draw date, because we do not have boots. Every promotion regime in the country requires prizes to actually be handed over, and Victoria requires it within 28 days of the draw.

Advertising a prize we cannot deliver is not a permit problem. It is misleading conduct, on a brand whose whole position is proof-first.

So either the prize is something we physically own on the day (a gift card, a crew pack of laces and rags and a coffee-van tab, a tool bundle bought at retail with the receipts kept), or the draw waits until stock lands. We do not draw for boots that do not exist.

The permit position, so nobody has to guess

Keep the total prize pool at or under $3,000 and we need zero permits anywhere in Australia. The ACT threshold is the lowest in the country and it is what binds us.

The seven conditions, all of them

  • Prize pool at or under $3,000. No permits anywhere.
  • The prize is something we physically hold on the draw date. Not boots, until boots exist.
  • Random draw from scans on a published date. Not a token in a box.
  • Email the terms to the WA regulator before it starts. Publish the winner online 28 days. Keep records 3 years.
  • Every placement permissioned in writing. A one-line text from the owner is enough. Photograph the yes.
  • Never name a retailer anywhere in copy, captions, terms or the landing page. Print "FAR is not affiliated with, sponsored by, approved by or endorsed by any retailer" on the box face and as clause 1 of the terms. No colour or typeface near anyone's branding.
  • Pilot 100 boxes, not 500, with a different QR per site so we learn which surface works. Only reprint at 500 if it beats 10% scan.

Boxes are quote-only from every Australian supplier. Minimum order 100, print from 250, about three weeks door to door, roughly $3 to $6 a unit, unverified. Get three written quotes before spending anything.

Round two: what the deeper dig turned up

Fil pushed back that the first round had only scraped the surface. Correct. Seven more research streams went in, hunting Pinterest, the practitioner forums, non-English sources and the operator accounts. 72 claims produced, 14 killed, 16 were duplicates of each other, 42 genuinely new tactics survive.

The dig was uneven and worth saying why. The English-language guerrilla marketing world was close to worthless: the big ad archive has no work-boot campaign in any medium, the design sites carry only logo work, and the famous marketing forum's "offline success stories" thread is a request for stories with no stories in it.

The gold came from places no marketer reads: German trades press, Japanese B2B procurement pages, a City of Cologne council tender annex, Korean commerce writing, Indian contractor-loyalty analysis, and Australian trade-supply catalogues.

Never. Two of these are actual crimes.

  • Nothing under a windscreen wiper. Ever. Offence in NSW, and whoever ASKS someone else to do it cops it worse than the bloke doing it. So Fil telling Grant to do it is worse than Grant doing it.
  • No stickers on pub walls, poles, hoardings or shop windows. Also an offence, same deal about whoever authorised it. The old plan had 200 stickers going into pub bathrooms and called it "surprisingly effective". That is dead.
  • No advertising trailer parked near a site. Banned since 2018. One ticket eats the whole budget.
  • No fake or seeded reviews. Not from us, not from family, not from a mate we gave free boots to, even if he genuinely likes them. An undisclosed freebie behind a review is illegal.
  • No sneaking our stuff onto a competitor's shelf. Activists get away with that because they are not in business. We are.
  • No buying four competitor boots and publicly daring them to respond. There is no case anywhere of a small brand successfully provoking a big one into replying. And a boot brand cutting open four rivals unavoidably implies ours is better, which we cannot prove.
  • No free sample to Rose Anvil. His whole position is that he BUYS everything he cuts. And it would be a sample, not production, so any fault the real run would have fixed gets attached to us forever.
  • No approaching Bunnings, Sydney Tools or the big buyers yet. A documented "no" from a national buyer follows us for years. Go back with sales numbers.
  • No comment-gates. "Comment BOOTS and I'll DM you." Every growth guide on earth recommends it. We do not do it.

And the honest maths on whether any of it pays

The whole printed layer might get us ten to forty signups. Our margin on a first-100 boot is about $130. So thirty signups only pays for itself if roughly a third of them actually buy, which a warm hand-delivered lead might do and a sticker on a hard hat definitely will not.

The small version is worth doing because the information is worth more than the leads. The full rollout is not worth doing until the small version comes back with a number.

And at these sizes we can only tell "some" from "none", nothing finer. A campaign expecting twelve responses could come back with eight or sixteen purely by chance.

Film the measuring, not the stunt

Grant pinning a poster, eight seconds. Thirty days later, Fil reading the real number out loud, whatever it is. If it is four scans, four scans is the video. A brand that publishes its own failed experiment earns something a brand that only ever publishes wins never can.

What the old plan got wrong

The old ground game document was written in March and never run. Five things in it were wrong or illegal.

  • Pub bathroom stickers. An offence, and it called them "surprisingly effective".
  • The 6:30am gate drops were assigned to Fil, who has no site access and no white card for those sites. That job is Grant's or it does not happen.
  • The FXD story we were copying is not what happened. They gave out shorts and socks, not boots, and they got in through a formal mentoring partnership, not by turning up.
  • Five thousand stickers with the wrong glue for hard hats, for a brand with nothing to own yet.
  • Blackwoods on the target list, now owned by Bunnings alongside two of our biggest competitors.

It also carried a dead materials claim in the poster and card copy that was about to be printed 5,000 times. That got caught in August, which is the only reason it did not end up on paper.
